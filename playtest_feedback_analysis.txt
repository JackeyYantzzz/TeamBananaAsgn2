In terms of operation comprehension, 7 participants thought the gameplay was easy to understand, and 1 thought it was extremely easy. No participant reported difficulty in understanding, indicating that the current operation guidance is effective.

Regarding the victory goals, 5 participants thought the goals were clear, and 1 thought they were very clear, but 2 still found the goals confusing. Participants could understand how to move and jump, but they may not have understood how to win the game.

There was a divergence in the difficulty evaluation. On the difficulty scale, 5 participants thought the difficulty was appropriate, 2 thought it was too simple, 1 thought it was slightly simple, and no one thought it was too difficult. In the gameplay perception test, the signal was more explicit: 7 participants said the game was very simple and could be easily completed, and only 1 thought it was slightly challenging and liked the challenge. Therefore, the actual experience of this prototype was generally easy.

The map balance received positive feedback. 7 participants thought it was basically balanced, 1 thought it was very balanced, and no participant thought the map was unfair. The geometric structure of the levels itself was not the main problem.

In terms of visual objects, 5 participants thought the character design was the best, 2 chose the map, and 1 chose the door. Trees, backgrounds, and fake diamonds did not receive votes. The character was the strongest visual focus, while the identification of the finish line and collectibles was relatively weak.

Regarding the willingness to play again, 5 participants chose "maybe", 2 chose "definitely", and 1 chose "probably". No one explicitly refused, but most participants had not yet formed a strong motivation to replay. The distribution of overall participation feeling was 1 point for 1 person, 2 points for 1 person, 3 points for 3 people, 4 points for 2 people, and 5 points for 1 person, with an average of approximately 3.1/5, at a medium and discrete level.

In the areas that need improvement the most, tower balance and attack effect/feedback each received 2 votes, operation, visual clarity, and game difficulty each received 1 vote. If the prototype includes defense towers or fixed defense points, tower balance should be addressed along with the difficulty issue; if this option comes from the questionnaire template and does not exist in the game system, this item should not be regarded as a design conclusion.

First, the difficulty is insufficient to support the participation experience. The vast majority of participants can complete the game without pressure, which is consistent with the willingness to play again being concentrated on "maybe" and the participation feeling remaining at a medium level. Without failure and room for mastery, clear rules do not automatically transform into playing motivation. At the same time, 5 participants still thought the difficulty was appropriate, so it is not advisable to uniformly increase the difficulty for the entire map.

Second, the communication of victory conditions is weaker than that of the operation. About a quarter of the participants were unclear about the winning purpose, and the door had almost no presence in the visual voting. The problem is more likely to lie in the target objects and target prompts, rather than the jumping teaching.

Third, insufficient action feedback weakens the significance of challenges and collection. Attack effects/feedback received relatively high improvement votes. In side-scrolling jumping, this problem covers jumping, landing, collection, being hit, and reaching the finish line, not just the attack animation.

Fourth, map fairness and character performance are current positive results, and do not need to be prioritized as a renovation target.

The difficulty should use a segmented curve, rather than a general increase. The front section retains the existing feel to confirm that jumping, landing, and collection can be understood; the middle section only introduces a new pressure, such as narrower landing points, moving platforms, enemies that need to wait for the rhythm to patrol, or time-limited collapsing platforms; the back section then combines the two mechanisms that have already appeared. The main path remains achievable while providing a shorter but more precise alternative route, and placing the fake diamonds and other collectibles on the risky route so that participants who already think it is appropriate do not have to be forced to accept a higher difficulty, and those who think it is too easy have a reason to replay. The checkpoints should be placed after each new mechanism, so that failure incurs a cost but does not result in the loss of the entire progress at once.

The victory conditions should be directly presented on the interface. At the beginning, a single sentence goal can be used, such as reaching a certain door or leaving after collecting a specified number of items, without the need to add a lengthy tutorial. The door should have an independent visual language, such as outline light, direction cues, and exit prompts when approaching. At the middle stage, a reminder of the goal can be given again to avoid collecting items being mistaken for the sole goal. If the fake diamond is not the victory condition, its brightness should not exceed the end point.

Action feedback should take precedence over further art re-drawing. Jumping can be accompanied by a slight squeeze, landing can include dust and a brief frame freeze; collecting can include fragmentation, digital changes, and short sound effects; being hit or stepping on an enemy can include a light tremor, flashing white, and recoil; the feedback when reaching the door should be longer than a normal collection to indicate that this is the end rather than another item. The character is already the visual focus, and there is no need to prioritize redoing the character.

Visual adjustments should serve the reading of the image. Hazardous objects need to be separated from the character and the ground, and should have a slight animation effect; the contrast of the door and the collectible should be higher than that of the trees and the background; the background can be further weakened to avoid competing for the edge with the platform. The fair layout of the map can be retained, and subsequent challenges should come from rhythm and feedback rather than overturning the existing geometry.

The operation only requires checking the feel. Current evidence does not support redoing the tutorial. You can check if jumping has a small input buffer and a wide landing tolerance, if there is limited horizontal correction allowed in the air, and if the attack will cancel the jump or get stuck on landing.

It is recommended to first supplement the victory goal prompts and the readability of the door to handle goal confusion; then complete the feedback for jumping, landing, collecting, and entering the door to enhance the sense of participation; then only add one new pressure in the middle or later stages and retain the optional risky path to respond to the issue of easy completion. If there are towers or fixed defense points in the game, adjust their output and the enemy durability relationship; otherwise, skip this item. The character performance and map fairness can be temporarily not changed.

The next round of testing can retain the original questionnaire and add two open-ended questions: where the participant first felt the challenge, and what they consider the winning method. If the proportion of "clearing without any difficulty" significantly decreases, and there is no longer confusion about the victory goal, then this round of modification can be considered effective. The willingness to play again shifts from "maybe" to "definitely", usually requiring improvements in both difficulty and feedback to complete this transformation.
